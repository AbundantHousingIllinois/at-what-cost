  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}Chicago's zoning code shapes far more than building heights and lot sizes: it determines who can afford to live here, where 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}economic opportunity takes root, and how equitably the city's future growth is shared. This report presents findings from a three-
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}year study by Metropolitan Planning Council, examining how zoning law and regulation have influenced housing availability and 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}affordability, business vitality, and adaptation to climate risks across the city. Grounded in the values of equity, sustainability, 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}and public health, the research draws on an extensive engagement process involving more than 700 individuals from over 450 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}organizations, including government agencies, community organizations, ward offices, and private sector partners.
[B 49,311,558,371]
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}The findings reveal a city constrained by its own regulatory framework. Just 11% of Chicago is zoned for multifamily housing{ProximaNova-Regular 5.2 #000000}1{ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}, while 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}41% is reserved for the most expensive and land-intensive housing type: detached single-family homes. The consequences of our 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}outdated zoning system are stark and geographically concentrated. For example, 60% of new housing built since 2006 has landed 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}in just five community areas, while 45 of Chicago's 77 community areas have added fewer than 500 new homes in nearly two 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}decades. Meanwhile, Black and Latinx residents have experienced sharp declines in affordability over the last two decades.

  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}These disparities extend beyond housing into the economic fabric of neighborhoods. Business concentration follows similar 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}patterns of inequity: majority-white census tracts average 14. 7 businesses per 1,000 residents, compared to just 8. 6 in majority-Black 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}tracts, a gap tied in part to how zoning allocates space for walkable, pedestrian-oriented commercial corridors. Zoning also has 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}tangible impacts on air, water, and land. Our research shows that Latinx Chicagoans make up 50% of the population in the top fifth 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}most air-polluted areas—and that Latinx neighborhoods tend to have more land zoned 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}for industrial use. These disparities pose both health and economic risks for Chicago’s 
  {ProximaNova-Regular 9.0 #000000}population.
