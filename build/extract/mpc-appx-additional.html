Appendix: Additional research findings

This section contains key research findings for two outcome analyses that were not included in the body of this report: productive land use, and available groceries and healthcare. To view comprehensive research findings for these and other outcome analyses, please visit the Zoning and Land Use Assessment webpage.

For more information on the data sources and methodology that informed the research findings below, please see APPENDIX: RESEARCH METHODOLOGY.

Productive land use

This outcome examined the relationship between infrastructure and density citywide. To understand infrastructure access, MPC developed an Urban Services Index, which combined measures to capture the quality of and the proximity to infrastructure across three categories: Transportation, Education, and Neighborhood Amenities. MPC also developed a Density Index based on population density, job density, and the amount of building space compared to developable land. These indices were calculated and assigned to block groups, which were then ranked by quartile. The rankings were used to identify different typologies related to infrastructure access and density (e.g. Low Infrastructure, Moderate Density).

Infrastructure access and quality are not universal, but each part of the city has infrastructural strengths. While disparities exist in public services across neighborhoods, every part of the city has strengths to support quality of life and continued growth. Using our Urban Services Index, MPC’s analysis indicated that the Northwest and Southwest sides showed strong scores on education infrastructure, the Central and West sides strong scores on transportation infrastructure, and the North and Southeast strong scores on neighborhood amenities. Top-scoring neighborhoods citywide for infrastructure access and quality include The Loop, Edgewater, Uptown, Kenwood, Near South Side, and Hyde Park.

There are dense places across the city, but the most dense places are in and near downtown and the North side. While dense areas exist across the city, density—measured through MPC’s Density Index, which captured population, job, and built-space density—is heavily concentrated near the urban core: roughly two-thirds of Chicago's densest areas are located downtown and on the North side. Not only do the Central and North regions contain the city's most dense areas, but the dense areas within these regions also have greater population, job, and built-space density than dense areas located in other regions of the city.

Chicago’s densest areas have a significant amount of low-density zoning. For places in Chicago with the highest levels of infrastructure and density, nearly one-third of land is zoned for low-density residential uses. This compares to 15% of New York City zoned for single family residential, or 0% in Portland. Our areas of existing infrastructural strength offers opportunities to support greater housing supply .

A significant portion of the city has more infrastructure than density. Areas classified as Moderate Infrastructure and Low Density comprise nearly 15% of Chicago. These are communities located largely across the West, Southwest, and Southeast sides, which have infrastructure to support more people working, living, and visiting. Gentle increases in density would support growth in these communities.

Available groceries and healthcare

Zoning does not appear to be responsible for disparities in healthcare and grocery access. Other factors like wealth, census tract demographics, antitrust enforcement, and health insurance access seem to be more related. Though zoning itself may not have caused these problems, there may be an opportunity to use zoning policies or other incentives to encourage the siting of groceries and healthcare in places that lack these key amenities.

The wealthiest and most diverse tracts tend to have the best access to primary care, as measured by city-identified primary care clinic locations and self-reported access to doctors. Except for the wealthiest and most diverse tracts, most tracts in Chicago have below the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended density of at least 1 physician practice per 1,000 residents. In 2021, just 20% of Chicago’s population lived in a tract with the WHO-recommended physician density. We also found stark gaps in access to pharmacies and the share of residents with private insurance, with both much more likely on the North side than other parts of the city..

Residents on the North side have greater access to healthy food options. While all census tracts have at least one food store in USDA’s SNAP food vendor database, the North side and parts of the West side are more likely to have food stores likely to provide healthier food options (grocery stories, supermarkets, superstores, etc.) in addition to food stores likely to have less healthy food (convenience stores, drug stores, and dollar stores). Poor grocery access for low-income households is strongly correlated with tract racial demographics, especially segregated Black communities.

Food stores overall are most likely to be located in areas zoned B3. At the same time, the larger food store types—superstores and supermarkets—are most often located within Planned Developments.

Chicago’s zoning ordinance widely allows both medical uses and grocery stores in business-oriented zoning districts, but does not explicitly incentivize these types of development. Medical services is a permitted use in all B and C zones, and all D zones except DR. Food and beverage retail sales is a permitted use in all B and C zones. Other cities use various strategies to incentivize grocery stores in overlay districts (e.g., Tulsa, OK); offer density bonuses to developments that include grocery stores (e.g. Carteret County, NC); or explicitly encourage healthy grocery access (e.g. Madison, WI).
